What a direct / non-relay exit is — and why it is not the same as relay or “dedicated”

What a direct / non-relay exit is should be read as a path type: how traffic leaves the exit, not as an ad that says “faster”. A relay adds a hop; a dedicated-line label is yet another tag. The three are not interchangeable. Opening the client and reading labels, latency and status is more useful than memorising jargon.

Three labels, one sentence each

High-speed direct

Closer to “one path out”. Whether it suits you still depends on the current latency, not on the name.

Dedicated relay

An extra hop on the path. A dedicated-line vs relay comparison is common, but relay does not always mean slower, and it does not always mean safer.

Premium dedicated

This is a label inside the Roxi client, not a certificate that the line is IPLC or CN2. What “dedicated line” means on a label cannot be reverse-engineered from an acronym.

Questions

What a direct / non-relay exit is — does it mean faster?

It describes a path, not a guarantee. When you compare direct and relay by latency and path, do it on your network, not from a slogan.

Direct vs relay: which is better?

There is no answer that holds for everyone. Even in the same region, the result can change by hour. Measure, then choose.

What “dedicated line” means on a label?

In Roxi you see tags such as “premium dedicated”. They mark a path type. They are not a backbone certificate and they do not replace a local speed check.

Is direct safer than relay?

Safety vs path type is easy to mix up. DNS leak protection, a kill switch, and keeping the subscription link on your own device usually matter more than the path name. You still need to verify routing, for example in the Karing custom-routing notes, the OpenClash router checks, or the SwitchyOmega migration notes.

Does Roxi record the sites I visit?

No. Whether you pick a direct or a relay label, Roxi does not keep browsing logs.
